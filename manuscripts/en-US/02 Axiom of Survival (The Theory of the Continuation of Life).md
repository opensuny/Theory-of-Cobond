## Chapter 1: The Ultimate Law of Life's Survival

Against the cold backdrop of the vast universe, the birth and death of stars follow the most ruthless laws of physics—from order to disorder, from intense heat to stillness. Yet, amid this irreversible torrent of increasing entropy, a tenacious "rebel" has emerged: life.

Earth’s epic history of life evolution, spanning billions of years, is a magnificent chronicle of the struggle for "survival." From the arduous fission of anaerobic bacteria in deep-sea hydrothermal vents to the dazzling bloom of the Cambrian Explosion; from the colossal forms of dinosaurs that dominated the continents to the tenacious intelligence of mammals rising from the margins—the invisible hand of natural selection has mercilessly wiped out those forms unable to adapt to their environments, yet has etched the instinct for survival into the very marrow of every survivor.
When apes first walked upright, when stone tools first struck sparks, the dawn of human origins began to break. Our ancestors rubbed sticks together to make fire, used knotted ropes to keep records, and lived in tribal communities—not for glory, but to secure one more thread of hope for survival in a harsh world beset by ferocious beasts and bitterly cold glaciers. Tribal totems and clan rules all revolved around hunting, procreation, and the defense of territory.

Later, villages grew into city-states, and city-states coalesced into nations. The walls of Babylon, the legions of Rome, and the postal roads of the Tang Dynasty—the most fundamental driving force behind the birth of these glorious civilizations remained, at their core, survival and order. Yet in the long river of history, the Hittites, the Assyrians, the Maya… countless once-mighty empires have crumbled to dust. On the surface, their collapse was attributed to barbarian invasions or natural disasters; at its root, however, lay the rigidity of their internal structures, which could no longer effectively serve the survival of their people.

Throughout the long history of human civilization, countless dynasties have risen and fallen, empires have flourished and declined, and political systems have emerged and vanished. While these events may appear to be power struggles, conflicts of interest, or clashes of ideologies, they all actually follow a timeless, indisputable underlying logic: survival is the primary purpose of life; continuity is the ultimate mission of civilization. This is the first axiom of *The Theory of Cobond*; it transcends class, national borders, and eras, and serves as the fundamental prerequisite for the existence of all forms of government, social systems, and political structures.

We need not seek evidence in obscure classical texts; we need only look back at the origins of humanity: from primitive tribes living in communities to defend themselves against wild beasts, to ancient city-states forming alliances to protect their homelands, and on to modern nations establishing order to safeguard the livelihoods of their people—the birth of every human organization has, at its core, been a response to a crisis of survival. When the strength of an individual is insufficient to withstand the threats of nature, invasions by external enemies, or resource scarcity, people choose to unite and coexist. This is an instinct etched into our genes and the very source from which civilization was born.
Biology: Worker bees "sacrifice" themselves to protect the hive; mother birds "pretend" to be injured to lure predators away and protect their chicks.
Sociology: Soldiers sacrifice themselves by blowing up bunkers or charging into the line of fire; firefighters rush into raging fires; doctors "march against the tide" during epidemics.
History of Civilization: Countless patriots and idealists have "sacrificed" themselves for national independence and the liberation of the people.

These sacrifices are not anti-life; on the contrary, they are strategic choices made by life itself to ensure its continuation on a broader scale and for the long term. Therefore, the "continuation" in the First Axiom does not refer to the immortality of the individual, but rather to the passing down of the torch within the community of life. Here, "life" refers not only to individual lives, but more broadly to the life of the community and the life of civilization. Thus, if an individual’s sacrifice can secure an opportunity for the community’s survival, it serves as a higher-level validation of the First Axiom.

The Axiom of Survival is the sole yardstick for evaluating the merits of all systems. A nation or a civilization—no matter how grand its slogans, how sophisticated its institutions, or how advanced its ideals—loses its reason for existence if it cannot provide its people with a safe living environment, stable livelihoods, and sustainable development opportunities, or if it cannot ensure the continuation of the flame of civilization. Conversely, even if a system is modest in form and simple in structure, as long as it safeguards survival and facilitates continuity, it is an excellent form that conforms to the laws of civilization.
This axiom also provides the most solid philosophical foundation for the emergence of the Gongbang model of civilization. Cobond is not a concept conjured out of thin air, but rather an adaptation to the instinct for survival and an adherence to the laws governing the continuity of civilization. It rejects the old models centered on the plundering of power and the jockeying for interests, and instead aims for mutual integration and symbiosis, enabling different entities to jointly withstand existential risks, share the fruits of development, and safeguard the flame of civilization within a relationship of equality.

Guided by the axiom of survival, we will come to see that all conflicts and divisions are, at their core, imbalances in survival needs; and all peace and harmony are, at their core, the alignment of survival aspirations. Cobond is precisely the optimal solution for resolving this imbalance in survival and achieving the sustainable continuity of civilization—it unites individuals and groups, as well as ethnic groups and nations, under a common goal of survival and enables them to move forward together in a shared destiny, ensuring that the great tree of civilization remains firmly rooted in the fertile soil of survival and continues to grow toward an eternal future. All systems, social structures, and ideologies must be evaluated based on this fundamental criterion.

Scientific Validity and Universality: This axiom holds absolutely true in biology and evolutionary theory. From single-celled organisms to humans, if survival is not possible, there can be no talk of culture, morality, or technology.
The Inevitable Struggle for Resources: Since survival is the paramount principle, and resources in the universe—matter, energy, and space—are finite, competition is inevitable. The Scale of Values: It reminds us that no matter how advanced a society becomes, "staying alive" will always be the highest-priority strategic consideration. This is neither a moral imperative nor a philosophical speculation, but an absolute command etched deep within every genetic helix. Whether it is a single cell, a solitary lion, a thriving empire, or a species gazing at the stars, the first axiom of its existence has never changed: the primary purpose of life is survival and continuity. ## Chapter 2: Earth as an Exam Hall: 4 Billion Years of Life and Death

If we were to compress Earth's 4.6-billion-year history into a single day, the exam paper for life would have been handed out at dawn. The earliest single-celled organisms marked the beginning of this long exam. They had neither fangs nor claws; their sole survival strategy was "primitive replication"—dividing and regenerating, relying on sheer numbers to combat death.

Yet the rules of the exam were cruel and unchanging: the environment changed, and the questions changed.

When oxygen began to accumulate in the oceans, it was no longer nourishment for anaerobic bacteria—it became a poison. Those early life forms unable to alter the "form" of their metabolism were permanently erased from the exam paper. Others, however, learned to utilize oxygen through endosymbiosis, evolving mitochondria and thereby gaining a highly efficient energy-production system. They passed the first round of elimination.
This is the harshest footnote to the theory of evolution: the essence of evolution is not becoming stronger, but becoming better adapted. When a species’ physiology, behavioral patterns, or social organization can no longer effectively support its survival in the current environment, extinction is not an accident, but a mathematical inevitability.

## Chapter 3: Fossils in Museums: Imprints of Frozen Morphology

Natural history museums display countless examples of "morphological function" reduced to zero.
**1. Dinosaurs: The Twilight of the Giants**
During the Cretaceous Period, massive bodies were a survival advantage for dinosaurs (form served the purposes of intimidation and resource acquisition). But when that life-altering asteroid struck Earth, and the sky-darkening dust brought a prolonged, bitter winter, their enormous size instantly became a fatal liability. The vast amounts of food required to sustain their massive bodies vanished, and they struggled to maintain their body temperature. Their morphology, which had earned a perfect score on the old exam, now scored zero on the new one. Meanwhile, the ancestors of mammals—smaller in size, with more varied diets, and greater adaptability—survived in the cracks of the ecosystem thanks to their agile "morphology."

**2. The Dodo: The Trap of Comfort**
On the isolated island of Mauritius, with no natural predators, the dodo’s wings had degenerated, leaving it clumsy and unafraid of humans. This morphology was energy-efficient and effective under the conditions of a safe, isolated island. But when humans—carrying ship rats—landed as a new environmental variable, the dodo’s morphology instantly became obsolete: it could neither fly away from danger nor defend against the invaders. Its morphology collapsed completely because it could not respond to the new security threats.

**3. Mammoths and Saber-toothed Tigers: The Dead End of Specialization**
They specialized their forms to the extreme—thick fur, long tusks, and razor-sharp teeth. When the Ice Age ended, forests replaced grasslands, and climate change altered the vegetation; these overly specialized forms could not adapt quickly enough, and ultimately disappeared along with the ecosystems on which they depended.
These extinct creatures teach us that no form is a permanent guarantee of survival. When a form loses its ability to adapt to the environment and ceases to serve the core functions necessary for survival, it is bound to disappear along with life itself.

## Chapter 4: Microscopic Miracles: The Symbiotic Covenant of the Superorganism
When we bend down to examine a blade of grass or gaze at the pistil of a flower, what we see is not chaotic strife, but precise cooperation.

**Ants and Bees: A Network of Unconditional Service**
In the kingdom of ants or the metropolis of bees lies the most primitive prototype of a living community.
Division of labor is service: worker ants forage, soldier ants stand guard, and the queen ensures reproduction. The physical form and behavioral patterns of each ant are not for its own pleasure, but for the survival of the entire colony. They share a pheromone network (communication system), share food reserves (economic system), and jointly repel external threats (defense system).
The Realm of Selflessness: Among these superorganismic creatures, the concept of the individual is diminished. From the perspective of morphological service theory, the death of a worker bee—if it ensures the hive’s safety—represents the maximization of the system’s service efficiency. They demonstrate that when individuals fully integrate themselves into a highly efficient service form, the probability of survival for the group (and its genes) increases significantly.

**Lion Prides and Wolf Packs: Fluid Offensive-Defensive Alliances**
On the African savanna, solitary lions struggle to capture wildebeest, whereas lion prides significantly increase their success rate through encircling hunts. This temporary, kinship-based "cooperative defense formation" serves the survival needs of every lion. The formation of a wolf pack in the snow serves both as a hunting formation and a structure for staying warm; every howl and position they take is intended to regulate the internal order of this small community to cope with the external cold and hunger.
These biological phenomena reveal a truth: form is a lever invented by life to overcome individual vulnerability. One end of the lever is the individual, and the other is the group; only when the group is strong can the individual be safer.

## Chapter 5: The Wisdom of Survivors: Morphological Leaps
Looking at species that have survived for hundreds of millions of years (such as sharks, cockroaches, and ginkgo trees), their success stems not from immutability, but from continuous morphological optimization and leaps.
The Evolution of Wolves and Dogs: Wolf packs survive in a harsh competitive environment by establishing strict social structures (hierarchy and cooperation); meanwhile, a subset of wolves altered their survival strategies by forming a symbiotic relationship with humans, evolving into dogs and leveraging the human "service system" to achieve a different kind of prosperity.

The Rise of Humans: We shed our thick body hair and developed a massive cerebral cortex. This was not merely a physiological change but an explosive evolution in organizational form. From loose kinship groups, we evolved into tribes and nations—these more complex structures provided stronger security, knowledge transmission, and resource allocation capabilities.

The history of life's survival is a history of architecture in which forms are constantly overturned, reorganized, and upgraded. Every drastic environmental upheaval serves as a major purge of existing forms; only those players capable of rapidly restructuring themselves—whether at the genetic or cultural level—to restore their functional efficacy can secure a ticket to the next round.

## Chapter 6: The Human Epic: The Convergence and Transition of Social Forms
The history of human evolution is a history of social form-building—the process of elevating biological instincts into civilizational institutions.
**Ancient Tribes: A Safety Net Woven from Blood Ties**
100,000 years ago, when our ancestors left Africa and faced saber-toothed tigers and harsh winters, the strength of a single individual was insignificant. Thus, the tribe was born. This was a primitive form based on blood ties. Men hunted collectively (sharing provisions), women gathered food and raised children (sharing the burden of care), and at night, they gathered around the campfire to share food and stories (sharing culture and mutual support). The core function of the tribal structure was to ensure the survival of every member—you watch my back, and I’ll share half a piece of meat with you. At this stage, the structure was simple, direct, and efficient.

**The Birth of the State: A Civilizational Vessel Forged by Contracts**
As the population grew and competition for resources intensified, conflicts between tribes (much like wars between ant colonies) became more frequent. To maintain order on a larger scale, repel external threats, and construct irrigation systems, a more complex form—the state—emerged. From the city-states of Sumer to the dynasties of China, the state, through laws, taxation, the military, and a bureaucratic system, provided a higher level of service: internally, it resolved disputes (judicial services); externally, it repelled invasions (national defense services); and it sought to provide relief to victims of natural disasters (social security services). Qin Shi Huang’s construction of the Great Wall, the Romans’ building of the Via Romana, and the Arab Empire’s establishment of postal stations—these monumental projects were, in essence, all aimed at optimizing the "provisioning" and "adaptability" of the state, to serve the survival and prosperity of (in theory) all subjects within the empire.
